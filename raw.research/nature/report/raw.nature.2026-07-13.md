## Synthesis — structural insight across all seven areas

The most important insight running across all seven coverage areas is that mycelium is not a passive conduit but an active, adaptive, resource-redistribution network whose architecture, signaling, and exchange properties are inseparable. The same hyphal strand performs structural, transport, electrical, and enzymatic functions simultaneously; the network's topology — looped, anastomosing, fractal — is precisely what makes those functions simultaneous rather than sequential. At every scale, from individual hyphal anastomosis (sub-millimeter, milliseconds) to Armillaria rhizomorphs spanning 2,200 acres over 8,000 years, the governing logic is the same: grow toward resource heterogeneity, fuse to form loops, redistribute toward shortage, and persist by minimizing division-rate in the growth front. Carbon moves toward light-limited sinks; phosphorus moves from chemically rich patches to root interfaces; water moves along hydraulic potential gradients; electrical spikes propagate at 0.5 mm/s at the moment of nutrient detection. These are not separate systems — they are concurrent modes of a single adaptive substrate.

## 1. Network Architecture and Structural Properties

### Hyphal organization

Mycelium is constructed from hyphae: cylindrical cells 2–20 μm in diameter that grow exclusively by polarized tip extension. This tip-growth mode is fundamental — the cell does not enlarge uniformly but elongates forward, depositing new cell wall material at the apex. Branching occurs behind the apex. Daughter hyphae initially display negative autotropism (avoidance of self) to maximize substrate coverage; secondary branch hyphae in colony interiors reverse this and show positive autotropism, growing toward one another and fusing by anastomosis. (The Mycelium as a Network, PMC11687498, 2024)

Anastomosis — hyphal fusion — is the mechanism that converts a branching tree into a looped network. The fusion process follows four steps: septum formation at the site of contact, initiation of growing hyphal tips, tip elongation and orientation, then fusion with restoration of cytoplasmic flux. Once formed, these loops create redundant pathways: if one hyphal strand is damaged, flow reroutes. Network resilience against localized damage is a direct consequence of anastomotic topology. (PMC9231406, 2022)

### Differentiated hyphal types

As networks mature, hyphae differentiate functionally. In cord-forming species, vessel hyphae emerge — 10–15 μm diameter, with reduced cytoplasmic contents and open lumen — that function as dedicated transport channels. In Armillaria and related species, hyphae aggregate into rhizomorphs: macroscopic shoestring-like organs 1–3 mm diameter with a melanized outer cortex and a transport-specialized inner core. Rhizomorphs extend at less than 3 meters per year and can persist dormant in soil awaiting new substrate. (Wikipedia: Armillaria ostoyae; PMC6304041, 2019)

### Fractal foraging geometry

Mycelial networks exhibit fractal space-filling organization. Mass fractal dimension (D_M) ranges from 1 for linear rhizomorphs (Armillaria) to near 2 for dense phalanx-type growths. This spectrum corresponds to two ecological strategies: "guerrilla" foragers (fast, effuse, low-density branching) optimized for rapid substrate discovery, and "phalanx" foragers (slow, dense, high-connectivity) optimized for thorough exploitation of located resources. The fractal architecture ensures that hyphal length per unit area remains roughly constant regardless of colony size — efficient coverage without redundant deployment. (PMC11687498, 2024)

### Transport physics within hyphae

Three transport systems operate at different distance scales:
- **Diffusion**: Small ions and solutes diffuse through the cytosol. A calcium ion crosses a 400 μm apical compartment in approximately 2 minutes; a protein requires ~2 hours. Diffusion is sufficient only for short intra-hyphal distances.
- **Motor-driven vesicular transport**: Microtubule motors move vesicles and organelles at 1–4 μm/s (14.4 mm/h maximum). Fungal microtubule motors are among the fastest known in biology. This system operates over distances of millimeters to centimeters.
- **Growth-induced mass flow**: The dominant long-range mechanism. As tip growth consumes osmolytes at the hyphal apex, a turgor pressure differential drives bulk cytoplasmic flow toward the tip. Measured velocities: nuclear movement at 5–20 μm/s in Neurospora; bulk transport at 27 cm/h in Suillus bovinus cords; up to 148 cm/h in Serpula lacrymans. These rates are orders of magnitude beyond what diffusion alone could achieve over centimeter-to-meter distances. (PMC11687498, 2024)

### Scale: the Armillaria ostoyae example

The Malheur National Forest specimen of Armillaria ostoyae covers 3.5 square miles (2,200 acres; 9.1 km²), weighs an estimated 35,000 tons (31,500 tonnes), and is estimated at approximately 8,000 years old. A Michigan Armillaria gallica specimen covers 75 hectares, weighs 400,000 kg, and is at minimum 2,500 years old. These individuals are genetically uniform clonal units — not aggregations of separate organisms — confirmed by identical multilocus genotypes across the entire spatial extent. (Wikipedia: Armillaria ostoyae; PMC6304041, 2019)

### Global scale

The upper soils of Earth contain approximately 110 quadrillion kilometres of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal hyphae, representing approximately 300 megatons of carbon in fungal biomass alone. Globally, terrestrial plants allocate an estimated 13.12 Gt CO2e per year to mycorrhizal fungal mycelium — approximately 36% of current annual fossil fuel CO2 emissions — making mycorrhizal networks a significant component of the terrestrial carbon cycle. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhiza; WebSearch: mycorrhizal mycelium global carbon pool 2023)

## 2. Cross-Species Exchange Mechanisms

### What is transported

Mycorrhizal networks transport a chemically diverse set of substances between connected organisms:

**Carbon (photosynthate)**: The primary currency. Plants transfer up to 30% of photosynthetically fixed carbon to fungal partners (Wikipedia: Mycorrhizal network). Interplant carbon transfers via common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) range from 0.02–41% of recipient plant carbon in experimental measurements, with dominant unidirectional flows and reverse fluxes generally below 15% of donor carbon. (PMC10369077, 2023)

**Nitrogen**: Transfer proportions between connected plants range from 0.04–80% of recipient plant nitrogen. The wide range reflects plant pairing — legume-to-non-legume pairs show highest rates (27–80%), while intraspecific transfers are lower. Nitrogen translocates as ammonium (NH₄⁺), not amino acids, indicating fungal enzymatic processing of organic nitrogen before translocation. (PMC10369077, 2023)

**Phosphorus**: Fungi acquire phosphorus from soil mineral weathering and organic matter decomposition, then deliver it to plant roots. As much as 80% of a plant's phosphorus can arrive via mycorrhizal pathways. Transfer is not passive — particle transport speeds within hyphae average 18.8 ± 11.8 μm/s, approximately 100× faster than Brownian diffusion, indicating active directed transport. When resource distribution is heterogeneous, fungal sections in phosphorus-poor patches consistently over-trade: they transfer more phosphorus per unit of fungal biomass, while sections in rich patches under-contribute — a behavior resembling market-price arbitrage across the network. (PMC6584331, 2019)

**Water**: Common mycorrhizal networks provide a physical pathway for water redistribution. Hydraulic redistribution via fungal hyphae can account for approximately 25–33% of daily evapotranspiration in some experimental systems. Apoplastic transport along hyphal walls, driven by water potential gradients, is the primary mechanism under drought conditions. Bioirrigation — transfer of hydraulically lifted water from deep-rooted to shallow-rooted species — has been demonstrated in legume-cereal pairings. (PubMed: hydraulic redistribution, PMID 17350936, 2007)

**Signaling compounds and secondary metabolites**: Networks carry allelochemicals (at concentrations 2–4× higher in connected versus isolated plants), salicylic acid and methyl salicylate (systemic acquired resistance signals), jasmonates, terpenoids, volatile organic compounds, strigolactones, and — recently demonstrated — messenger RNAs, noncoding RNAs, and proteins. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhizal network; Annual Review of Plant Biology, Shen et al., 2023)

**Flavonoids**: AM fungal hyphae carry flavonoids as signals coordinating symbiosis establishment, with eight flavonoids unique to compartments containing extraradical hyphae. (ISME Journal, He et al., 2024)

### Directionality and conditions

Transfer direction is dynamic, not fixed. Carbon flows toward photosynthetically limited sinks — empirical evidence: in spring, newly budding birch receives carbon from green Douglas fir; in summer, shaded Douglas fir understory seedlings receive carbon from birch in full leaf; in fall, birch again receives from Douglas fir as birch senesces. This seasonal reversal demonstrates active source-sink allocation rather than passive diffusion. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhizal network)

The carbon-phosphorus exchange rate at the symbiotic interface is tightly coupled — rates of carbon transfer from plant and phosphorus transfer from fungus are, on average, proportional. Recent work using robotic imaging and machine learning with ~100 million hyphal shape measurements quantified this exchange rate constraint as a key determinant of fungal growth dynamics (PNAS 2025).

Carbon acts as a trigger for fungal nitrogen uptake and transport — increasing carbon supply increases the rate at which the fungus acquires and delivers nitrogen to the plant. (Plant Signaling & Behavior, Fellbaum et al., 2012)

### What each partner contributes and receives

Arbuscular mycorrhizal plants transfer ~20% of their photosynthate to fungal partners in exchange for equivalent phosphate amounts. AM fungi are obligate biotrophs — entirely dependent on host carbon for survival, having lost all saprotrophic enzymatic capability. Ectomycorrhizal fungi, by contrast, retain some saprotrophic enzyme repertoire. EcM fungi gained an additional predatory nitrogen acquisition route: Laccaria bicolor lures and kills springtails (Collembola), with Eastern White Pine deriving up to 25% of its nitrogen from springtails via this mechanism. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhiza)

## 3. Chemical and Electrical Signaling Pathways

### Chemical signaling

Chemical signaling operates through multiple parallel pathways:

**Systemic acquired resistance (SAR) priming**: Aphid-infested plants transmit signals through mycorrhizal networks that allow uninfected neighbors to pre-activate salicylic acid (SA) defense pathways before herbivore attack. This defense priming has been demonstrated for both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal networks. Allelochemical concentrations in receiving plants reach 2–4× those in isolated plants. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhizal network)

**Pathogen alerts**: Tomato plants connected via AM networks display defensive priming when neighbors face fungal pathogen attack — the signal traverses the network before any direct contact with the pathogen.

**Stress signaling**: Defoliation of Douglas fir triggers carbon transfer and defense signaling to neighboring ponderosa pine through ectomycorrhizal networks — the 2015 Simard lab finding demonstrating that resource reallocation and defense signaling co-occur during stress. (Simard Lab, 2015)

**Strigolactones**: Plant-derived hormones produced in roots under phosphorus limitation, signaling to AM fungi and triggering fungal hyphal branching toward root surfaces. The AM symbiosis signaling cascade is now substantially characterized: strigolactone → fungal branching factor → plant symbiosis genes activation → arbuscule formation. (Plant Journal, Mashiguchi et al., 2021)

### Electrical signaling

Fungi generate action potential-like electrical signals through redistribution of ions across hyphal membranes. Ion channels involved include voltage-gated calcium channels (Cch1), proton pumps, potassium channels, and chloride channels. These are functionally analogous to — but mechanistically distinct from — animal neurons: in fungi, negative current injection increases signal amplitude, whereas in neurons it inhibits. (FEMS Microbiology Reviews, PMC11995700, 2025)

Key measurements:
- Spontaneous action potentials in Armillaria bulbosa and Pleurotus ostreatus: 0.5–5 Hz frequency, 5–50 mV amplitude
- Rhizomorph and mycelial strand action potentials: frequency increases upon nutrient detection
- Signal propagation velocity: 0.5 mm/s over distances of centimeters when nutrients were added remotely
- Measured currents at hyphal tips: 0.05–0.60 μA cm⁻² (low intensity, distinct from millivolt-range neuronal signals)

For comparison: plant electrical signals propagate at 5–25 cm/s in Venus flytrap; animal nerve signals at 0.1–100 m/s. Fungal propagation is substantially slower but still rapid relative to chemical diffusion.

**Plant-to-plant electrical communication**: A 2022 study (PubMed PMID 36384396) demonstrated that electrical signals are reliably conducted across mycelial bridges from one plant to another upon wound induction. Pea and cucumber plants connected by fungal bridges (grown across air gaps to exclude all non-fungal pathways) showed measurable voltage changes in millisecond-to-second timescales at the receiving plant. This represents a third plant communication channel, distinct from root secretion and volatile emission.

**Key distinction from nutrient transport**: Electrical signaling continues even when septal pores are blocked (preventing cytoplasmic flow and chemical signal transmission) — suggesting electrical and chemical signals are distinct systems with partial independence. Additionally, inward electrical currents at hyphal tips reflect proton-driven nutrient symport (the coupling of proton and nutrient co-transport), creating a measurable electrical signature of active uptake that is mechanistically tied to metabolism, not communication. Researchers must carefully distinguish these two sources of electrical signal. (PMC11995700, 2025)

### Response latency comparison

| Signal type | Mechanism | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| Diffusion (small ions) | Brownian | 400 μm in ~2 min |
| Motor-driven vesicle | Microtubule motor | 1–4 μm/s |
| Mass flow in cords | Pressure-driven | 27–148 cm/h |
| Action potential in hyphae | Ion redistribution | ~0.5 mm/s |
| Chemical diffusion (allelochemicals) | Network flux | hours to days |

## 4. Symbiotic Dependency Structure

### Structural types

**Arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM)**: Fungal hyphae penetrate plant cell walls and form highly branched structures called arbuscules within cells (without penetrating the cell membrane — arbuscules form between the cell wall and the plasma membrane, creating a large contact surface). AM fungi belong exclusively to the phylum Glomeromycota, with approximately 300–1,600 species identified. They associate with approximately 78% of all plant species and 85% of plant families. AM fungi are generalists with minimal host specificity. They are **obligate biotrophs**: entirely dependent on plant-derived carbon, having lost all saprotrophic enzymes. This obligate dependency is total — AM fungi cannot complete their lifecycle without a host. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhiza)

**Ectomycorrhizae (EcM)**: Fungal hyphae do not penetrate cells; instead they form a hyphal mantle around the root tip and a Hartig net penetrating between (not into) cortical cells. EcM associations involve 20,000–25,000 fungal species but only ~6,000–7,000 plant species (approximately 2% of plant species), almost exclusively woody plants in Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae, Dipterocarpaceae, and Myrtaceae. EcM fungi evolved polyphyletically from free-living saprotrophs and retain some saprotrophic enzymatic capacity, enabling evolutionary reversal to saprotrophic lifestyles. They are **facultatively symbiotic**. (Wikipedia: Mycorrhiza)

### Dependency asymmetries

- AM fungi are fully obligate on plant carbon; plants are not obligate on AM fungi (many plants grow, though less efficiently, without AM colonization)
- Inoculation experiments show aboveground biomass increases of 51–174% with AM inoculation, but plants are not uniformly obligate
- Some plant families (Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Proteaceae) are non-mycorrhizal by evolutionary loss
- EcM tree species (pines, oaks, beeches) show strong dependency in nutrient-poor soils — pine seedling establishment without EcM fungi is severely limited in boreal forest conditions
- The Aster amellus system demonstrates local adaptation between plant genotypes and specific fungal strains — not merely obligate dependency but co-adapted pairs

### Network-scale structure

Mycorrhizal networks in Douglas fir forests exhibit scale-free topology: most young trees connect to large, old "hub" trees that are disproportionately linked. Hub trees (corresponding to Simard's "mother trees") function as central nodes. Network topology is resilient to random loss of participants but vulnerable to targeted removal of hub trees or dominant fungal genets. This is the characteristic vulnerability profile of scale-free networks. (Beiler et al. 2015, Journal of Ecology; WebSearch: network topology 2024)

## 5. Saprotrophic and Decomposition Roles

### What is decomposed

Saprotrophic fungi are the primary decomposers of lignocellulosic plant material — the main structural compounds in dead plant tissue: lignin (the aromatic polymer providing mechanical strength), cellulose (the glucose polymer in plant cell walls), and hemicellulose (the branched polysaccharide matrix). Saprotrophic fungi also decompose proteins (to amino acids), lipids (to fatty acids and glycerol), and starches. (Wikipedia: Saprotrophic nutrition)

### White rot vs. brown rot mechanisms

**White rot fungi** (Basidiomycota: Trametes versicolor, Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Pleurotus ostreatus, Irpex lacteus) are the only organisms that completely degrade all three lignocellulose components. Their enzymatic arsenal:
- Lignin peroxidases (LiP): oxidatively cleave aromatic rings in lignin via H₂O₂-dependent radical mechanism
- Manganese peroxidases (MnP): oxidize Mn²⁺ to Mn³⁺, which then diffuses into the wood matrix and oxidizes phenolic compounds
- Versatile peroxidases (VP): combine LiP and MnP activities in a single enzyme
- Laccases: copper-containing oxidases that catalyze one-electron oxidation of phenolics using O₂ as terminal electron acceptor
- Dye-depolymerizing peroxidases (DyPs): supplementary oxidoreductases
- Cellulases: endoglucanases (EG) cleave non-crystalline cellulose regions; cellobiohydrolases (CBH) digest chain ends; β-glucosidase (BGL) hydrolyzes cellobiose to glucose
- Quantitative example: Irpex lacteus degraded 74.9% lignin, 86.3% cellulose, and 83.5% hemicellulose in corn stover within 9 days under experimental conditions. (PMC5833081, 2018)

**Brown rot fungi** (Basidiomycota: Postia placenta, Serpula lacrymans) degrade cellulose and hemicellulose but modify rather than fully degrade lignin. Mechanism: non-enzymatic Fenton chemistry — Fe²⁺ ions react with H₂O₂ to generate hydroxyl radicals (·OH) that cleave cellulose chains randomly. These radicals diffuse into cell walls ahead of the fungus, pre-digesting the substrate. Lignin is demethoxylated and partially depolymerized but not mineralized — brown rot residues are characteristically brown, friable, and lignin-enriched. (WebSearch: lignocellulose decomposition enzymes)

### End products

End products of complete saprotrophic decomposition: CO₂ (from lignin and cellulose oxidation), water, inorganic nitrogen (ammonium, nitrate), phosphate, and mineralized micronutrients. These are released into the soil and become available for plant uptake — directly via mycorrhizal fungi in many cases. Saprotrophic fungi thus close the nutrient cycle that photosynthesis opens. The distinction from mycorrhizal function is categorical: mycorrhizal fungi obtain carbon from living plant photosynthate; saprotrophic fungi obtain it from dead organic matter. Some EcM fungi retain limited saprotrophic ability and can access nitrogen from soil organic matter — blurring the boundary. (Wikipedia: Saprotrophic nutrition; Wikipedia: Mycorrhiza)

## 6. Reproduction Lifecycle

### Spore to mycelium

Spore germination requires: temperature 15–35°C (optimum 20–25°C for most species); relative humidity 90%+; substrate pH approximately 5.5–7.0; presence of nutrients or host-derived signals (especially for AM fungi, which require strigolactone detection). At the optimum, germination initiates within hours; away from optimum, germination slows and germ-tube elongation is retarded. (WebSearch: spore germination conditions)

A germinated spore produces a monokaryotic (single nucleus per cell) mycelium that cannot reproduce sexually. Monokaryotic mycelia are limited in function — they can grow and persist but most cannot form fruiting bodies.

### Dikaryotization and network establishment

When two genetically compatible monokaryotic mycelia make contact, they fuse (a process also mediated by anastomosis). The result is a dikaryotic mycelium — two nuclei per cell, from two different mating types. Dikaryotic mycelium can persist indefinitely in vegetative growth, expanding by tip extension and anastomosis. This is the dominant growth phase in forest soil systems — the vast majority of the mycelial network a fungus occupies is dikaryotic vegetative mycelium.

### Fruiting body formation

Fruiting body development is triggered by environmental signals that indicate favorable conditions for spore dispersal. The developmental program proceeds through five stages: (PMC12351640, 2025)

1. **Hyphal aggregation**: Transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. Cell surface proteins called hydrophobins facilitate aerial hyphae aggregation. Triggered by nutrient depletion and environmental stress signals (nutrient sensing via GPCRs, Ras, cAMP, MAPK pathways).
2. **Primordium formation**: Dense hyphal knots form, usually under hormonal and environmental stressor influence. Blue light (400–500 nm) at approximately 2000 lux activates transcription factors for primordium initiation.
3. **Mycelial extension and maturation**: High carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N) initiates early fruiting; later stages require low C:N. Primordia expand.
4. **Fruiting body morphogenesis**: Hyphal knots differentiate into cap, stalk, and spore-producing structures (gills, pores, or other hymenophore forms). STRIPAK complex and MAPK signaling coordinate tissue differentiation.
5. **Spore formation and dissemination**: Meiosis produces haploid basidiospores (or ascospores). Dispersal typically via autolysis in some species; wind dispersal from epigeous fruiting bodies; zoochory (dispersal by mammals) for hypogeous (truffle-like) species.

**Environmental thresholds**: Temperature optimum 15–27°C; CO₂ must drop from 10,000–20,000 ppm (during spawn run) to ≤1,000 ppm (preferably 500–800 ppm) for pileus formation — fruiting is suppressed by elevated CO₂. Oxygen minimum: 0.5% pO₂ to initiate primordia; 1% pO₂ for mature fruiting body formation. Humidity 80–95%.

**Genetic regulation**: HMG-box transcription factors and epigenetic mechanisms (histone acetylation H3K9ac, H4K16ac; DNA methylation; small RNAs) regulate the developmental transition. The vegetative-to-reproductive switch involves massive transcriptional reprogramming. (PMC10282164, 2023; PMC12351640, 2025)

### Spore dispersal

Wind-dispersed basidiospores are released in enormous quantities — single Ganoderma fruiting bodies can release billions of spores over a season. Dispersal distances range from centimeters to thousands of kilometers for wind-carried spores. Hypogeous fungi (truffles, Rhizopogon) depend on fungivorous mammals (squirrels, deer, voles) for dispersal — the spores pass through the digestive tract intact and are deposited in feces. Rhizopogon spores dispersed in this way have been identified as a key mechanism for mycorrhizal recolonization after forest disturbance. (Wikipedia: Sporocarp)

## 7. Persistence and Age Scales

### What determines lifespan

Mycelium does not have a fixed lifespan in the sense that individual hyphae die and are replaced continuously; the organism as a clonal genetic unit can persist indefinitely as long as:
1. **Substrate availability**: New organic substrate is accessible within reach of rhizomorphs or extending mycelium
2. **Absence of competitive exclusion**: No competing fungal individual displaces it from its substrate
3. **Genomic stability**: Accumulation of deleterious mutations remains below fitness-threatening thresholds

### Armillaria: mechanisms of millennial persistence

The Armillaria gallica individual C1 (Michigan, ~2,500 years, 75 ha, 400,000 kg) displays four documented mechanisms of genomic stability:

1. **Tip cell pool dilution**: Rhizomorph tips may contain hundreds to thousands of genetically distinct cells; any new mutation in one cell is diluted against the remainder, reducing fixation probability by orders of magnitude
2. **Quiescent meristematic strategy**: Growth occurs primarily by elongation behind the apex, not by tip cell division — similar to plant root meristems, which also show exceptionally low mutation rates. The entire 75-hectare expansion accumulated only ~163 genetic variants across 14 strains
3. **Enhanced DNA repair**: Selection over 2,500+ years may have purified repair enzyme activity
4. **Asymmetric DNA strand segregation**: Cells committing to long-term lineage propagation receive older (more tested) DNA strands; cells committing to local development receive newly replicated strands

Mutation rate estimate: approximately 20 mutations per km of spatial separation — implying roughly one cell division per meter of growth. This is dramatically lower than hyphal density per meter would predict, suggesting most of the rhizomorph structure is post-mitotic and growth is highly localized. (PMC6304041, 2019)

### Survival between resource patches

Rhizomorphs can persist in soil **dormant** during conditions unfavorable for growth — low substrate, drought, cold — then resume growth when new substrate becomes available. This persistence mechanism is structurally different from spore dormancy: it is a living, metabolically suppressed network that retains the capacity to resume growth without germination. The melanized cortex of the rhizomorph likely provides protection against desiccation, UV, and microbial attack. (Wikipedia: Armillaria ostoyae; PMC6304041, 2019)

### Age determination methodology

For Armillaria: genotyping by multilocus markers (microsatellites, SNPs) across all spatial extent to confirm genetic identity; age estimated from expansion area and measured growth rates (~3 m/year maximum for rhizomorphs). Clonal identity confirmed by identical mitochondrial markers between samples collected 25+ years apart.

For the 8,000-year Oregon A. ostoyae estimate: size (2,200 acres) divided by maximum growth rates. This is a lower-bound estimate with substantial uncertainty — actual age could be higher if growth rates were lower in past climate conditions.

### Comparison: mycelial network lifespan vs. fruiting body lifespan

The NATURE article "Lifespan and functionality of mycorrhizal fungal mycelium are uncoupled from host plant lifespan" (Scientific Reports, 2018) established that the mycelium of mycorrhizal fungi persists after host plant death — the network does not depend on a single host for continuity. This uncoupling allows the network to persist across tree generations, potentially serving as a bridging structure for seedling recruitment in the next cohort.

### Scientific disputes and open questions

**The "wood wide web" oversimplification**: Wikipedia's mycorrhizal network article explicitly flags that "many of the claims made about common mycorrhizal networks, including that they are ubiquitous in forests, that resources are transferred between plants through them, and that they are used to transfer warnings between trees, have been criticised as being not strongly supported by evidence." Key uncertainties: whether carbon transfer through CMNs materially affects plant fitness (versus being a metabolic byproduct of fungal activity); whether observed interplant chemical signaling has measurable ecological effect in field conditions versus laboratory setups.

**Direct vs. indirect nutrient transfer**: Whether nutrients flow through hyphal "pipelines" directly between plants, or whether fungi take up nutrients from soil and release them near roots (without continuous hyphal connection carrying plant-to-plant flow) remains unresolved for many nutrient classes.

**Electrical signaling functional role**: The 2025 FEMS Microbiology Reviews paper concludes that "definitive proof of electrical signaling's biological function and role in fungal communication remains elusive." Action potential-like signals are documented; what they accomplish biologically is not. The confound between ion channel-driven signaling signals and proton-nutrient symport currents (metabolic noise) is unresolved.

**Signaling latency**: No source provided direct measurements of response latency for the full signaling chain — from stimulus (e.g. herbivore attack on one plant) through the network to behavioral change in a connected plant. The 2022 electrophysiology study measured voltage propagation; the ecological studies measured gene expression changes at timescales of hours. The intermediary signaling dynamics are not mapped.

**Hyphal growth unit universality**: The hyphal growth unit constant assumes isotropic growth; in differentiated cord and rhizomorph systems this breaks down. Transport models for mature mycelial cord networks lack full experimental validation at field scales.

**Arbuscular mycorrhizal species diversity**: Estimates range from ~300 to ~1,600 AM fungal species depending on morphological vs. molecular species concepts. Molecular phylogenies are still being resolved.

**Age estimates for large Armillaria**: The 8,000-year estimate for A. ostoyae (Malheur) is based on size-to-growth-rate calculation and assumes constant radial expansion and survival — neither is guaranteed over geological timescales. It is a plausible lower bound, not a confirmed age.
